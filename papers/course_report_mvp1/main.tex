\begin{abstract}
% src: structure from the owner (data, prior work, why, result, future work), 2026-11-18; sentences from paper Sections 1, 2 and 7 (D-052)
The Polymarket-v1 database records every trade on Polymarket from 2022 to 2026.
Studies of horse racing and earlier prediction markets find that cheap bets win less often than priced.
Readers take a Polymarket price as a probability, so we test whether it is one.
On \NMarketsKept{} resolved Yes/No markets, we ask three questions: is the crowd right, who is the crowd, and what do trades look like?
Prices are not calibrated as a whole, but the gap is small and runs one way: events happen slightly less often than priced.
A few wallets hold most of the trading volume and take most of the winnings.
Trade sizes are strongly right skewed, so the median describes a typical trade better than the mean.
Future work can correct prices after the fact and test the correction on later markets.
\end{abstract}

\hd{5. Conclusion}
Polymarket prices are close to probabilities, but events happen slightly less often than priced (Q1).
A few wallets dominate: the top \TopTwentyLabel{} hold \MakerTopTwentyShare{} of maker volume (Q2).
The top 1\% of wallets take an estimated \PnlTopOnePctShareWon{} of all winnings.
A typical trade is small: the median is \TradeSizeMedian{} USDC, the mean \TradeSizeMean{} USDC (Q3).
A reader can use a price as a probability if they read it a little lower.

\hd{6. Limitations}
% Owner asked for the limits only, without numbers (2026-11-18); the numbers stay in the paper (check M3)
\textbf{Independence.} Our tests assume that markets are independent.
Markets on one event move together, so the p-values may be too small.
The data hold no event key, so our check with a proxy key is only partial.
\textbf{Selection.} Only a small share of markets pass the filters, and kept markets trade more than dropped ones.
% Price day and wallet limits copied from ../paper_mvp1/sections/12-limits.tex (owner, 2026-11-18)
\textbf{Price day.} Trading continues after the scheduled close, when the answer may be known.
So we price one day before the close, but this choice rests on a one-month pilot.
\textbf{Wallets.} A wallet is an address, and one owner may control many addresses.

\hd{7. Future work}
% Volume is already tested in Appendix C, so the next step holds volume fixed (owner, 2026-11-18)
Each limitation leads to one next step.
Build a true event key, measure how markets on one event move together, and test Q1 with markets grouped by event.
Q2 shows a concentrated crowd, but we do not link it to Q1.
So test whether the gap depends on the number of distinct wallets, holding volume fixed.
Correct prices after the fact and test the correction on later markets.

\hd{Appendix C. Size of the Q1 gap}
\textbf{C.1 Gap against the trading cost.}
We fit a logistic calibration of the outcome on the price, $\mathrm{logit}\,P(y=1)=a+b\,\mathrm{logit}(p)$.
The fit gives an implied gap, fitted probability minus price, at each price.
The practical line is the mean half spread, \RThreeMOneMeanHalfSpread, fixed before the run.
The half spread is known for \RThreeMOneHalfSpreadN{} kept markets, and it is skewed: its median is only \RThreeMOneMedianHalfSpread.
So the mean line is a high bar, and a typical market costs less to trade.
The largest implied gap on the price grid is \RThreeMFiveMaxAbsDelta{} in size.
Of \RThreeMFiveGridPrices{} grid prices, \RThreeMFivePracticalPrices{} have their whole \CiLevel{} band past the practical line.
So the gap is clear in the statistics, but we cannot say it is surely larger than the mean half spread.
We set no size line before the results, so ``small'' is our reading.

\textbf{C.2 Gap by market volume.}
We split the kept markets into three equal groups by volume before the price day.
The cut points are \RThreePeerACutLowMid{} and \RThreePeerACutMidHigh{} USDC.
The net gap is \RThreePeerALowNetGap{} in the low group (\CiLevel{} confidence interval, CI: [\RThreePeerALowNetGapCiLow, \RThreePeerALowNetGapCiHigh]).

\hd{Appendix E. Correcting for ten bin tests}
We test each of the \NBins{} bins, so one bin could look significant by chance.
We correct for this in two ways and report both.
\par\smallskip
% Steps as numbered lists (owner, 2026-11-18)
\textbf{Bonferroni.} It controls the chance of even one false finding.
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep0pt
\item Run the exact binomial test in each bin. This gives ten p-values.
\item Split the 0.05 level over the ten tests: one cut, $0.05/10 = 0.005$.
\item Reject a bin when its p-value is under 0.005.
\item Result: four bins, 0, 7, 1 and 6.
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{Benjamini-Hochberg (BH)} \citep{benjamini1995controlling}. It controls the expected share of false findings among the findings.
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep0pt
\item Run the exact binomial test in each bin. This gives ten p-values.
\item Rank the p-values from smallest (rank 1) to largest (rank 10). The rank uses only the p-value, not the bin number or the price.
\item Give rank $i$ the cut $i \times 0.05/10 = i \times 0.005$, so the cut grows with the rank.
\item Find the largest rank whose p-value is under its cut. Here it is rank 5 (bin 8, $0.018 < 0.025$). Rank 6 (bin 3) misses ($0.058 > 0.030$).
\item Reject every bin up to that rank, even one that misses its own cut.
\item Result: five bins, the four above and bin 8.
\end{enumerate}
