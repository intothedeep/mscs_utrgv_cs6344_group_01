Question 1 of 4 · Amanda Aninagyei

Do you use the price from a set time before the market closes?

Asked: "Are you planning to use the price from a set time before the market closes?"

Yes. We use the price 1 day before the scheduled close. We fixed this rule before we looked at any result.

  1. Take the market's scheduled close time, close_at. Call it T.
  2. Find the last trade at or before T − 1 day.
  3. Several trades can share that timestamp (one block). We average their prices.
  4. If that price is more than 24 hours old, we drop the market. A stale price is not today's crowd view.

Figure 1. Where the evaluation price sits in a market's life. Why: a price taken after the close can already contain the answer.

If we used "the last trade" as the end, every market that keeps trading after its close would get a later evaluation point. That is 51.3% of the pilot markets. Their price could then contain news from after the scheduled end, and calibration would look better than it is. Source: runs/142041_pilotA_2025_06/close_time_summary.json.

We also take a second price at T − 7 days (see Question 3).

Question 3 of 4 · Luis Tapia

What about prices that swing wildly right before the close?

Asked: "...or prices that swing wildly right before a market closes?"

We avoid the last day on purpose, and we compare two horizons.

  • Our main price is from 1 day before the close, not the closing minutes.
  • A market needs at least 10 trades before that point. A price from two trades is noise.
  • We compare the price 7 days before the close with the price 1 day before, on the same markets.

Question 4 of 4 · Luis Tapia

Does the favorite-longshot bias change by topic?

Asked: "Do you think the favorite-longshot bias changes much depending on the topic, like politics versus crypto?"

Yes. Cheap bets in Crypto and Politics pay off less often than priced. In Sports they do not.

Figure 3. Markets priced 10 to 20 cents one day before close: mean price against the share of events that happened, by topic. Why: a blue bar below the grey bar means the cheap bet paid off less often than its price said.

  • Same direction as horse racing: longshots are overbet and pay too little (Snowberg and Wolfers, 2010).
  • Topic matters overall too: how often the favorite wins differs by topic (chi-square test of independence, Cramér's V = 0.258, p < 0.0001). Sports favorites win 72.2% of the time, Crypto favorites 94.8%.
  • Caution: markets on one event move together, and the test assumes they do not. We report this as a limit.

Full results: report v2 (MVP1 + MVP2). Data: Qin and Yang (2026), Polymarket-v1 Database, arXiv:2606.04217. Longshot bias in horse racing: Snowberg and Wolfers (2010), Journal of Political Economy 118(4): 723–746.
