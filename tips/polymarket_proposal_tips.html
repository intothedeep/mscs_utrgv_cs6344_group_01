Step 1 of 7

Why this question

Problem
People read a Polymarket price as a chance. Nobody can check one price alone, because one event happens only once.
Idea
Collect many markets with the same price. Count how often their events happened.
Mission
Show, from real trades, whether a price can be read as a chance.

A YES share pays $1 if the event happens. It pays $0 if it does not. So a share at $0.70 is read as a 70% chance.

You buy one YES share atEvent happensEvent does not happen
$0.70You get $1.00 (gain $0.30)You get $0.00 (loss $0.70)

Polymarket-v1 makes the check possible. It holds 1.2 billion trades on 1.3 million markets, November 2022 to April 2026. Most markets have a known winner. Only a small subset lacks one.

Step 2 of 7

How to check a price

A price is calibrated when events priced at p happen about p of the time. We put markets into price bins (0.0–0.1, 0.1–0.2, and so on). For each bin, we compare the mean price with the share of events that happened.

Figure 1. A calibration plot, one point per price bin. Illustrative values. Why: one picture shows every price range at once.

The plot alone is not proof. A point can sit off the line by chance. So each bin also gets a test.

  • Binomial test, one bin: 100 markets priced near 0.70 should give about 70 events. Is 62 too far from 70 to be chance?
  • Chi-square test, all bins: do all ten bins together fit the line?
  • Confidence interval (CI): a range for the true share in each bin. A small bin gets a wide range.
Step 3 of 7 · References 1 and 2

Longshots: cheap bets that pay less than their price

A longshot is an outcome with a low price, such as a horse at long odds. A favorite has a high price.

WhereWhat the reference shows
Horse racing [1]"Long shots are overbet whereas favorites are underbet." Longshot horses win less often than their odds say.
Polymarket [2, Table 3]Tokens priced 0.00–0.30 pay out slightly less than their price. Tokens priced 0.40–1.00 pay out slightly more.

Figure 2. Mean return per token by price decile, from the dataset paper, Table 3. Mean return = payout − price. Why: the sign flips near price 0.4, so cheap tokens and expensive tokens err in opposite directions.

The gap is small. In the 0.0–0.1 decile, the mean price is 5.2 cents. The mean return is −0.23 cents. So a token bought for 5.2 cents paid back about 4.97 cents on average.

The dataset paper calls this a "reversal" of the horse-racing bias. Snowberg and Wolfers say longshots are overbet, so they pay too little. Polymarket longshots also pay too little. The direction is the same, so we call it the same bias.

What we add: we test the same gap on Polymarket with our own bins and tests (Step 2).
Step 4 of 7 · Reference 2

Count markets, not trades

Table 3 counts each trade once. The first decile alone has 34,902,814 trades. A busy market has many trades, so it gets many votes. Its one outcome then decides much of the bin.

Figure 3. The same 10 markets counted per trade and per market. Illustrative values. Why: per trade, one busy market outvotes the other nine.

What we add: one price per market, one outcome per market. Then each market is one vote. This answers "how often do events priced at 70% happen?"
Step 5 of 7 · Reference 2

The Brier score: one number for accuracy

The Brier score is the squared gap between the price p and the outcome y. y is 1 if the event happened, else 0. Lower is better. 0 is perfect.

Brier = (p − y)²

Price pOutcome yBrierReading
0.70happened (1)0.09Close: (0.70 − 1)² = 0.09
0.70did not (0)0.49Far: (0.70 − 0)² = 0.49
0.50either0.25A coin flip. A useful price scores below 0.25 on average

Who sets the prices

A maker posts an order and waits. A taker accepts a waiting order. The paper measures each wallet's lifetime volume.

Source: dataset paper, Table 5.

Figure 4. Lorenz curve sketch for makers. The dot (99%, 15.9%) comes from Table 5. The curve shape is a sketch. Why: the gap between the curve and the dashed line is what the Gini measures.

If a few wallets trade most of the volume, the price may be their view. Then "the crowd" is a small crowd. The paper reports the concentration. It does not link it to accuracy.

What we add: we ask if a few wallets set the prices. We redraw the Lorenz curve, compare our Gini with 0.970, and group wallets by behavior.

Three traps we plan around

TrapWhat goes wrongWhat we do
Trades after the answer is knownThe price already holds the outcome. Calibration then looks perfect.Measure the close time on a pilot first. Take the price 1 day before close.
Nearly empty marketsA price from two trades is not a crowd view.
  • Set a minimum trade count before we see results.
  • Report results with and without it.
Linked marketsMany markets on one election move together. The tests assume independent markets.Test per topic, and state this limit in the report.
