Q: How do you use the z score in Q1?

Only for intervals: z = 1.96 sets the 95% interval in each bin. The bin test itself is a binomial test.
How: rate r̂ = outcome-1 count / n. Interval = r̂ ± 1.96 · √(r̂(1 − r̂) / n).
Example: n = 2,301, price 74.6%, rate 71.3%. 1.96 · 0.0094 ≈ ±1.85 points.
The interval misses the price in 5 of 10 bins (0, 1, 6, 7, 8), the same bins the Benjamini-Hochberg test marks.
Source: paper Section 4.2 and 5.1; run 142082 q1_bins.csv
Backup, Q1 speaker. About 45 seconds. Why: someone may ask where the z score comes in. We use it only to draw the 95% interval in each price bin. The test that says a bin differs from its price is a binomial test, not a z test. How: in a bin we take the outcome-1 rate, r hat. Its standard error is the square root of r hat times one minus r hat, over n. The interval is r hat plus or minus 1.96 standard errors, and 1.96 is the z value for 95%. Example: the 70 to 80% bin has 2,301 markets, a mean price of 74.6% and a rate of 71.3%. The half width is about 1.85 points, so the gap of minus 3.29 points has an interval from minus 5.14 to minus 1.44. The whole interval is below 0, so events in this bin happen less often than priced. Result: the interval misses the price in 5 of 10 bins, the same bins the Benjamini-Hochberg test marks. Two cautions: the interval has no correction for 10 bins, and it assumes independent markets.
