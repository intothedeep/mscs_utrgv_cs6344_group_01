Q: Why report both BH and Bonferroni?

Bonferroni: p < 0.005 BH: p < rank × 0.005
Bonferroni: 4 bins
Guards the chance of even one false find. Cut: 0.05 / 10 = 0.005 for every bin. Strict.
BH: 5 bins
Guards the share of false finds among the finds. The cut grows with rank. Less strict.
Only bin 8 differs. The strict rule still keeps 4 bins, all priced too high. The answer does not depend on the choice.
p-value axis: log scale. Source: run 142082 q1_bins.csv; paper Section 5.1
Backup, Q1 speaker. About 45 seconds. Why: we run ten tests, one per bin, so by chance alone one could look significant. We must correct for that, and there are two common ways. How: the chart sorts the ten p-values from smallest to largest on a log scale. A bin is significant when its point falls below a line. Bonferroni, the dashed line, guards the chance of even one false find. It uses 0.05 divided by 10, so 0.005 for every bin. That is strict. Benjamini-Hochberg, or BH, the orange step line, guards the share of false finds among the finds. Its cut grows with the rank: rank times 0.005. That is less strict. Result: Bonferroni keeps 4 bins: 0, 7, 1 and 6. BH keeps 5. The only difference is bin 8, with p equal to 0.018. It falls below the BH line but above the Bonferroni line. Meaning: even under the strict rule, 4 bins remain, and all of them are priced too high. So our answer does not depend on which correction we pick. The paper reports BH as the main result and Bonferroni as a stricter check.
